\section{SAO}

\subsection{Algorithm}
To address the policy lag issue, SAO~\citep{hou2026single} proposes several modifications over the PPO-Clip objective to reach the following objective:

\begin{align}
     \label{eq:sao.obj}
     \max_{\theta}\;
     \Jcal_{\text{SAO}}(\theta)
     :=
     \Embb_{\tau \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{rollout}}}}
     \left\{
         \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
         \left[
             \operatorname{clip}\left(
                 \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)}
                      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{rollout}}}(a_t \mid s_t)},
                 1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},
                 1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}
             \right)
             \hat{A}_{\phi}(s_t,a_t)
         \right]
         \log\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)
     \right\},
\end{align}
where~\eqref{eq:sao.obj} is written in REINFORCE form~\eqref{eq:reinforce.obj}: the clipped importance ratio times the advantage is treated as a detached weight, and the explicit $\log\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)$ term provides the policy-gradient direction.

\textbf{Remark}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item SAO drops the proximal policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and uses the behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{rollout}}}$ for importance sampling:
    \[
        r_t(\theta)
        =
        \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t\mid s_t)}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\text{rollout}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
    \]
    This also avoids recomputing probabilities under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ by reusing the log-probabilities generated during rollout.

    \item SAO applies a different trust region by clipping the rollout ratio to $[1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}]$. As shown in \autoref{fig:sao.clip.region}, PPO clips by asking ``Has this sample already moved too far in the direction that appears beneficial?'', while SAO clips by asking ``Is the current policy still sufficiently close to the policy that generated this token for this sample to be trustworthy?''.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{PPO's asymmetric construction is useful} when the advantage estimation is reliable, the data is fresh, the large ratio arose from parameter coupling or prior minibatch updates, and we want the sample to help undo an undesirable policy shift. For example, if $A=-1$ and $r=10$, PPO-Clip keeps the directionally corrective term $rA=-10$, which pushes the sampled action's probability down, while SAO clips it. Similarly, if $A=1$ and $r=0.1$, PPO keeps the corrective gradient, while SAO clips it. Because only one side is saturated for each advantage sign, PPO generally uses more of the batch and supports repeated minibatch optimization over collected data. However, PPO's retained corrective gradients can become dangerous in asynchronous or heavily off-policy training.
        \item \textbf{SAO's symmetric construction is helpful} in asynchronous or heavily off-policy training. For $A<0$ and $r\gg1$, the factor $rA$ can dominate a minibatch. That may be acceptable if $A$ is accurate and the sample is still relevant, but it can be destructive when the sample was generated by a substantially older actor, the trajectory context is highly off-policy, or one extreme token dominates the aggregate gradient. SAO bounds the importance ratio regardless of the advantage sign, giving direct control over this source of gradient heavy tails.
    \end{itemize}

PPO clips only in the regions selected by the clipped surrogate objective, while SAO directly clips the rollout-ratio $r=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{rollout}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ to $[1-\epsilon_{\text{low}},1+\epsilon_{\text{high}}]$.}
        \label{fig:sao.clip.region}
    \end{figure}

    \item The strict two-sided gate has costs. First, once a token is outside the band, it gives no direct gradient, even when its advantage would have produced a restoring update. This is a distinction between filtering and constraining: SAO filters data; it does not project or roll the policy back into a feasible region. Second, importance sampling is unbiased only without truncation under the usual assumptions. PPO introduces bias through one-sided clipping, while SAO introduces more bias by dropping both tails. In particular, dropping a negative-advantage outlier can make the observed surrogate look artificially favorable because a large negative contribution has disappeared. Therefore, SAO's gated objective does not have PPO's uniform pointwise pessimism property. Third, clipping reduces the effective batch size: if many tokens leave the band, the nominal batch size may remain large while the number of contributing tokens collapses.

    \item The paper does not specify every detail of the advantage estimator $\hat{A}_{\phi}$. We sketch a consistent token-level interpretation below.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Guessed Advantage Estimator}



\paragraph{Trajectory, action spans, and states.}
Let $P$ denote the fixed task context supplied before interaction begins. It may contain system instructions, user instructions, task metadata, and chat-template tokens preceding the environment state. Before the agent takes its first action, the environment provides an initial observation $o_0$. The interaction then proceeds as
\[
    o_0 \xrightarrow{a_0,\,R_0} o_1
    \xrightarrow{a_1,\,R_1} o_2
    \cdots
    \xrightarrow{a_{M-1},\,R_{M-1}} o_M.
\]
Equivalently, the chronological trace is
\[
    P,o_0,a_0,o_1,a_1,\ldots,o_{M-1},a_{M-1},o_M.
\]
Here $o_i$ is the observation available before action $a_i$; $a_i$ is the $i$-th model-generated action span; after executing $a_i$, the environment returns reward $R_i$ and next observation $o_{i+1}$; and $o_M$ is the final observation after the last sampled action. 
Action $i$ consists of $n_i$ policy-generated tokens:
\[
    a_i=(a_{i,0},a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,n_i-1}).
\]
These are exactly the tokens sampled from the policy during the $i$-th assistant generation. Tokens inserted by the chat template, such as an assistant-role marker, are not included in $a_i$ unless they are actually sampled by the policy. Similarly, observation tokens are not part of $a_i$ because they are supplied by the environment rather than generated by the policy.

Let $\sigma_i$ denote the serialized assistant prefix placed immediately before generation of action $a_i$. Depending on the chat template, this may include tokens such as \texttt{<assistant>}, or tool-call formatting tokens. Define $c_i$ as the complete token sequence available immediately before the first generated token $a_{i,0}$. Concretely,
\begin{align*}
    % c_0:=\operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,\sigma_0).
    c_i
    :=
    \operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,a_0,o_1,a_1,\ldots,a_{i-1},o_i,\sigma_i) \text{ for } i\ge 0.
\end{align*}
    



For the $j$-th token of action span $i$, define $a_{i,<j}:=(a_{i,0},\ldots,a_{i,j-1})$.
The policy-decision state before generating token $a_{i,j}$ is $s_{i,j}:=c_i\Vert a_{i,<j}$, where $\Vert$ denotes token-sequence concatenation. Thus $s_{i,0}=c_i$. The token-generation rule is
\[
    a_{i,j}\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_{i,j}),
\]
and the value associated with the same pre-token state is
\[
    v_{i,j}:=V_\phi(s_{i,j}).
\]
This notation keeps the actor and critic aligned: both \textbf{evaluate the prefix before the token is sampled}.

\paragraph{Successor states.}
Inside action span $i$, for $j<n_i-1$, the next decision state is
\[
    s_{i,j+1}=s_{i,j}\Vert a_{i,j}.
\]
At the end of the action span, the agent emits $a_{i,n_i-1}$, the environment executes the completed action, and returns $o_{i+1}$. The next policy-decision state is
\[
    s_{i+1,0}=c_{i+1}.
\]
Expanded,
\[
    s_{i+1,0}
    =
    \operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,a_0,o_1,\ldots,o_i,a_i,o_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1}).
\]
The complete action $a_i$, not merely its final token, determines the environment transition. The final token is simply the token-level decision to which the action-level reward $R_i$ is conventionally aligned. Observation $o_{i+1}$ is part of the next decision state, but individual observation tokens are not separate policy-decision steps.

For two action spans, the causal structure is
\[
    P,o_0,\sigma_0
    \xrightarrow{\text{generate }a_0}
    a_0
    \xrightarrow{\text{environment}}
    R_0,o_1,\sigma_1
    \xrightarrow{\text{generate }a_1}
    a_1
    \xrightarrow{\text{environment}}
    R_1,o_2.
\]
The corresponding policy-decision sequence is
\[
    s_{0,0},s_{0,1},\ldots,s_{0,n_0-1},s_{1,0},s_{1,1},\ldots,
\]
where
\[
    s_{0,0}=\operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,\sigma_0),
    \qquad
    s_{1,0}=\operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,a_0,o_1,\sigma_1).
\]
This is the sequence on which skip-observation GAE should operate. The observation $o_1$ is part of $s_{1,0}$, but its individual serialized tokens are not separate policy-decision steps.

\paragraph{Transformer and value-head alignment.}
Let
\[
    x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_{T-1}
\]
be the complete serialized token sequence, including prompt tokens, observations, action tokens, role tokens, tool delimiters, and assistant-prefix tokens. Let $p_{i,j}$ be the physical position of action token $a_{i,j}$, so
\[
    x_{p_{i,j}}=a_{i,j}.
\]
By the state definition,
\[
    s_{i,j}=x_{<p_{i,j}}.
\]
Write the value model as a causal transformer body followed by a scalar head:
\[
    h^V_t=F^{\mathrm{body}}_\phi(x_{\le t}),
    \qquad
    \bar v_t=g^{\mathrm{head}}_\phi(h^V_t),
\]
with $\phi=(\phi_{\mathrm{body}},\phi_{\mathrm{head}})$. The raw output $\bar v_t$ evaluates the prefix through physical position $t$:
\[
    \bar v_t=V_\phi(x_{\le t}).
\]
Since the state before action token $a_{i,j}$ is
\[
    s_{i,j}=x_{<p_{i,j}}=x_{\le p_{i,j}-1},
\]
its value is
\[
    v_{i,j}=V_\phi(s_{i,j})=\bar v_{p_{i,j}-1}.
\]
This means:
\begin{itemize}
    \item before an interior action token, the relevant hidden state is usually located at the preceding action token;
    \item before the first token of a new action span, the relevant hidden state is located at the final serialized token before generation begins;
    \item that final preceding token may be an observation token, tool delimiter, or assistant-role token.
\end{itemize}

For the first token after observation $o_{i+1}$,
\[
    v_{i+1,0}=V_\phi(s_{i+1,0})=\bar v_{p_{i+1,0}-1}.
\]
Because $s_{i+1,0}=x_{<p_{i+1,0}}$ contains $o_{i+1}$, the critic prediction $v_{i+1,0}$ is conditioned on the complete next observation.

\paragraph{Value output mask.}
Suppose \texttt{action\_mask[$p$]} is $1$ exactly when $x_p$ is a model-generated action token. The value output at physical position $t$ is used when the next token $x_{t+1}$ is an action token:
\[
    \texttt{value\_loss\_mask[$t$]}
    =
    \texttt{action\_mask[$t+1$]}.
\]
This detail matters. One should not simply discard every critic output located at an observation-token position. The output at the final observation or assistant-prefix position may be exactly the value needed for the next action's first token. The rule is: keep critic outputs representing states before generated action tokens, and ignore critic outputs representing states before non-generated tokens.

\paragraph{Reward alignment.}
Let $r_{i,j}$ be the reward assigned to the transition caused by choosing token $a_{i,j}$. Inside an action span, the usual assignment is
\[
    r_{i,j}=0,\qquad j<n_i-1,
\]
unless token-level shaping rewards are used. If the environment returns reward $R_i$ after executing the complete action span $a_i$, assign it to the span's final token:
\[
    r_{i,n_i-1}=R_i.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    r_{i,j}
    =
    \begin{cases}
        0, & j<n_i-1,\\
        R_i, & j=n_i-1.
    \end{cases}
\]
For a trajectory with only one terminal verifier reward $R$, all token rewards are zero except the final token of the final action span. This does not claim that the final token alone caused the reward; earlier action tokens receive credit through the backward GAE recursion.

\paragraph{Token-level TD residuals and advantages.}
For an interior token $j<n_i-1$, the next policy-decision state is $s_{i,j+1}$. Therefore,
\[
    \delta_{i,j}:=r_{i,j}+\gamma v_{i,j+1}-v_{i,j},
\]
and the GAE recursion is
\[
    A^{(\lambda)}_{i,j}:=\delta_{i,j}+\gamma\lambda A^{(\lambda)}_{i,j+1}.
\]
Normally $r_{i,j}=0$ for interior tokens, so $\delta_{i,j}=\gamma v_{i,j+1}-v_{i,j}$.

For the final token of a nonterminal action span, the next decision is $a_{i+1,0}$ after observation $o_{i+1}$ has been incorporated into the context. Thus,
\[
    \delta_{i,n_i-1}:=R_i+\gamma v_{i+1,0}-v_{i,n_i-1},
\text{ and }
    A^{(\lambda)}_{i,n_i-1}:=\delta_{i,n_i-1}+\gamma\lambda A^{(\lambda)}_{i+1,0}.
\]
% Expanded in terms of serialized contexts,
% \[
%     \delta_{i,n_i-1}
%     =
%     R_i
%     +
%     \gamma V_\phi\!\left(\operatorname{Serialize}(P,o_0,a_0,o_1,\ldots,a_i,o_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1})\right)
%     -
%     V_\phi(c_i\Vert a_{i,<n_i-1}).
% \]
This is the well-defined version of the paper's expression connecting the last token of one action to the first token of the next action. The observation tokens are included in the next state, but no separate GAE steps are inserted for each observation token.

\textit{Final token of a terminal episode}. Suppose action span $M-1$ terminates the episode. There is no next policy-decision state, so set $v_{\mathrm{terminal}}=0$ and $A_{\mathrm{terminal}}=0$. Then
\[
    \delta_{M-1,n_{M-1}-1}:=R_{M-1}-v_{M-1,n_{M-1}-1},
\]
and
\[
    A^{(\lambda)}_{M-1,n_{M-1}-1}:=\delta_{M-1,n_{M-1}-1}.
\]
This is the initialization point for the backward recursion in a completed episode.

\textit{Truncated but nonterminal trajectory}. A time limit, maximum-token limit, or context limit is not necessarily a true terminal state. Let $s_{\mathrm{boot}}$ be the valid policy-decision state from which the rollout would have continued, and define
\[
    v_{\mathrm{boot}}:=V_\phi(s_{\mathrm{boot}}).
\]
For the last sampled action token,
\[
    \delta_{\mathrm{last}}
    :=
    r_{\mathrm{last}}+\gamma v_{\mathrm{boot}}-v_{\mathrm{last}}.
\]
Since no later TD residuals were sampled, initialize the GAE carry as $A_{\mathrm{next}}:=0$, giving
\[
    A_{\mathrm{last}}:=\delta_{\mathrm{last}}.
\]
The bootstrap value represents the unobserved future return. Setting $v_{\mathrm{boot}}:=0$ for an ordinary truncation would incorrectly treat the truncation as failure or termination.

\subsection{Practical Considerations}

\paragraph{What to do?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Faster Value Update than Policy. The authors attribute primary source of instability in single-rollout RL is the interdependence between the policy and the value function. If the value model $V_{\phi}$ is inaccurate, the advantage estimates $\hat{A}_{\phi}(s_t,a_t)$ become noisy, leading to destructive policy updates. Specifically, for every single gradient update applied to the policy $\pi_{\theta}$, we enforce $K$ updates to the value network $V_{\phi}$ (default $K=2$).
    \item Stabilizing Value Model Training via Parameter Freezing. Instability of value model training, where the gradient norms of the value model are significantly larger than the corresponding policy model. Further decomposition shows that this instability originates primarily from the Full Attention layers. As a result, the authors freeze the parameters of the Full Attention layers during value model training.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What to monitor?}
A strong hybrid is often:

\begin{center}
     gross-staleness gate+PPO or soft rollback within the gate+explicit KL/TV monitoring.
\end{center}

For diagnosing whether the extra SAO masking is helping, the most informative measurements are not merely the aggregate clip fraction. Track the four A-by-r quadrants separately, the accepted-token fraction by actor version, importance-weight effective sample size, log-ratio quantiles, and the gradient norm contributed by the $A<0,r>u$ quadrant. That last quadrant is where SAO is most likely to gain stability and where it sacrifices the strongest potentially corrective PPO signal.
